---
title: "After Reading geohot’s Posts from 2020–2026"
date: 2026-11-15
---

After reading these posts written by geohot, what stayed with me is not any single article, but a single recurring standard: almost every judgment eventually comes back to whether something concentrates power or disperses it.

He rarely evaluates technology in moral or emotional terms. Instead, he keeps asking a more concrete question—who still holds real control after everything is done. Things that put capability into more people’s hands tend to look good to him, and things that lock capability into a small number of organizations tend to look dangerous. The criterion is almost blunt, but once you start applying it, it explains a lot: why he dislikes platform lock-in, why he dislikes unnecessary complexity, and why he dislikes handing safety entirely to a small group of “trusted” authorities.

One feeling came through strongly while reading: he would rather live with chaos than with perfectly managed order. A system that can patch every vulnerability and solve every coordination problem is not, in his view, an ideal. It is a cage that becomes harder to escape. Order itself is not the problem; the problem is when order becomes permanently bound to power that can no longer be contested. That is why he can say he would rather have anarchy than tyranny—not because he likes anarchy, but because anarchy at least still contains variables.

On technological progress, his standard is equally clear: real progress should lower costs, lower barriers, and make capability portable. Open source, local control, and cheap compute are, to him, ways of spreading capability. The opposite path—making something pleasant first, then gradually locking users in and raising switching costs—he largely rejects. He does not believe AI will wipe out the human species, but he does worry that it will freeze existing social and power structures in place, or rebuild them in a worse form.

Toward people themselves, his view is relatively detached. He tends to see humans more as biological machine-learning systems than as traditional subjects with souls or free will. This distance shows up when he talks about consciousness, universities, managerial classes, and even democracy. In a world where the value of labor falls sharply but voting rights remain, he is not optimistic—voting power itself can become a lever for manipulation rather than a safeguard.

I did not finish these posts in full agreement. Some of the judgments feel too absolute, and the preference for chaos carries obvious risks. But the writing does offer a perspective that is relatively rare: it does not start from “this should be better,” but from “where will control ultimately sit.” In a conversation dominated by safety, alignment, and efficiency, the persistent focus on control itself stands out.

If I had to sum up the feeling in one line: he is not describing an ideal future so much as repeatedly pointing out that once capability becomes concentrated to the point it can no longer be dispersed, many arrangements that currently look reasonable will become irreversible.
