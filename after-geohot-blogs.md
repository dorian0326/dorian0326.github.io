After reading these posts written by GeoHotz, what stayed with me is not any single article, but a single recurring standard: almost every judgement eventually comes back to whether something concentrates power or dispress it.

He rarely evaluates technology in moral or emotional terms. Instead, he keeps a more concrete question - who still holds real control after everything is done. Things that put capability into more people's hands tend to look good to him, and things that lock capability into a small number of organizations tend to look dangerous. The criterion is almost blunt, but once you start applying it, it explains a lot, like: Why he dislikes platform lock-in, why he dislikes unnecessary complexity, and why he dislikes handing safety entirely to a small group of "trusted" authorities.

One feeling came through strongly while reading: he would rather live with chaos than with perfectly managed order. A system that can patch every vulnerability and solve every coordination problem is not, in his view, an ideal. It is a cage that becomes harder to escape. Order itself is not the problem, the problem is when order becomes permanently bound to power that can no longer be contested. That is why he can say he would rather have anarchy than tyranny - not because he likes anarchy, but because anarchy at least still contains variables.
